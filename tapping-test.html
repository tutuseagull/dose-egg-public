Tapping test

Alternating finger tapping, fifteen seconds a hand. Measures speed, rhythm, the decrement across the trial, and hesitations — the upper-limb relative of freezing. Pure touch events: no motion permission, no https requirement, works on any phone. Open on a phone.

Why this one, and not more tremor. This is the digital form of UPDRS item 3.4, and it reaches things tremor does not: bradykinesia, rhythm breakdown, and the sequence effect, where movement gets progressively smaller and slower the longer you keep going. Those track with a freezing-dominant presentation far better than tremor does. It is also the cheapest instrument in the whole set to build and the most reliable to measure.

The instrument

Tap the two circles alternately with one index finger, as fast and as evenly as you can. Do one hand, then the other. Rest your forearm.

What each number is

  • Rate — taps per second. The blunt bradykinesia measure.
  • Rhythm variability — the coefficient of variation of the gaps between taps. Irregular timing is a PD signature independent of being slow, and someone can be fast and irregular.
  • Decrement — the fitted slope of interval against tap number, in milliseconds lost per tap, plus the change from the first third of the trial to the last. This is the sequence effect. A healthy hand holds roughly steady; a parkinsonian hand fades.
  • Hesitations — gaps longer than twice the median. These are the halts, and they are the closest thing on a touchscreen to freezing.
  • Accuracy — mean distance from target centre as a fraction of the target radius. A dysmetria proxy, and the weakest of the six.
What we cannot measure, and it matters. UPDRS 3.4 grades amplitude decrement, the thumb and index finger opening less and less. On a screen the distance between targets is fixed, so we only see the temporal decrement. Related, not the same thing, and any write-up has to say so rather than borrow the clinical scale's authority.

This one is closer to a report card than the tremor test, and that is a real risk. Tremor happens to you. Tapping is something you perform, so it can be done well or badly, and a person will feel graded whether or not we show a grade. Two consequences. The result state shows no number, same as before. And we must never show the person their own tapping history over time, because a declining line on a progressive condition is the single most harmful chart we could draw.
The practice effect will fool us before it fools anyone else. People get better at the task itself over the first several sessions. That improvement is learning, not health, and it looks exactly like a symptom improving. Any comparison across time has to discard early trials or it is measuring familiarity with a phone game.

What to try

  • Both hands. Asymmetry is the interesting part. PD is asymmetric at onset, so a gap between hands is more informative than either hand alone.
  • Deliberately fade. Start fast and let yourself slow down. Watch the decrement number move. That proves the metric responds to the thing it claims to measure.
  • Deliberately stall. Pause for a second mid-trial. That is a hesitation, and it should be counted.
  • The question for the domain lead. Not whether the numbers look right, but whether a minute of tapping is something she would actually do on a stiff day, and whether being asked to perform feels different from being asked to report.
