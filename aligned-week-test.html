One week, one axis

We had been designing each logging category its own picture. This is the other thing: every dimension on the same time axis, so the correlations are visible rather than asserted. One week only — the scale where detail is affordable.

Tutu, 2026-08-27. "It makes sense for each logging category to have its own visualization and summary, but what could be more interesting is to visualize all dimensions based on the same time axis, so the user can actually see the correlations… we are helping the user connect the dots." And: "let the chart tell the story" — fewer statements, not more.

The week

Two groupings of the same week. By day — tap a day; the target is the whole row and where you land sets the hour. By measure — four charts stacked on one axis; tap a part of the day.

What the chart is doing

Feeling is the ground, not a lane

A rough patch is a period, not an event — so it is drawn as a soft span behind the whole day, crossing every other lane. That is the same figure/ground move we landed on for coverage, one level down, and it is what makes the correlation legible: you don't compare a "feeling row" against a "dose row", you see the dose sitting inside the rough patch or outside it.

Sleep is a band, anchored at its end

Sleep crosses midnight, so it belongs to the day you woke on. The band enters from the left edge and fades, meaning "this started before the window" — the duration is stated rather than measured off the axis, because measuring a clipped bar is a lie.

Add-ons are a treatment, not a second mark — and there are two states, not three

Per D-16 a slot is one ask, so it is one mark: a ring around the dose dot means the slot included the add-on, and no ring means it didn't. I had drawn a third state, a broken ring for "add-on skipped". Tutu: "I don't think we need add-on skipped. User would say with or without add-on."

The distinction underneath is worth keeping. "Skipped" is a comparison against the plan, which the app computes. "With / without" is what the person actually said. Drawing the computed one turns the chart into a compliance check — the report card wearing a third hat, and this time in a place we would not have looked for it. The plan deviation is still recoverable from the data if we ever deliberately want it; it just isn't something the picture should volunteer.

Two groupings, two questions

Tutu: "could we fit 3 or more charts on one screen, stacking style, but share the same x-axis?"

Yes, and it is a different chart rather than a tidier one. Same data, transposed:

  • By day groups by day and puts the dimensions inside it as lanes. It answers "what did Tuesday look like". The correlation is read across lanes within a row.
  • By measure groups by dimension — one panel each, seven day-rows inside, all on one shared axis. It answers "when do doses happen" and, crossing panels, "does the rough stretch always land at the same hour". The correlation is read down a column across panels.

The second is the stronger layout for finding a pattern and the weaker one for reading a single day, which is why both are here. It also keeps the figure/ground move alive through the transpose: the rough stretches ghost into every panel, so you can see a dose sitting inside one without leaving the dose panel.

The stack changes what a target is — and that is C-17 working

By measure there are twenty-eight rows of 11px on screen. No row can be a target, and that is fine, because the unit worth interrogating here isn't a day at all — it's a time of day. So the target becomes a part of the day: a full-height column, 44px wide at its narrowest on a 390pt phone, and the readout it produces is the sentence this layout was built to support — "Early afternoon — 6 doses, 5 with an add-on, 2 high-protein meals, and 2 of the 3 rough stretches begin here."

Which is the accessible / sensible / narrative test doing real work rather than confirming a choice already made. The target changed because the zoom changed; the chart never stopped responding.

The statement is secondary

One quiet line under the chart, and the coverage line under that. The chart is doing the work; the sentence only names what you are already looking at.

What I can't settle here. Three things need real data or a real person. One: whether four lanes is already too many on a 320pt phone — meals is the first candidate to drop, since protein matters to timing and timing is visible in the dose lane anyway. Two: whether the rough-span ground reads as an observation or as a verdict on the day — the same trap as the report card, one level down. Three: whether people read the vertical alignment at all without being told; the whole design assumes they scan down a time column, and that is an assumption, not a finding.

Still open

  • Activity has no lane. Walks, exercise, drumming — currently they would have to live in the meal lane as generic events, which muddles it. Either a fifth lane, or events and meals merge into one "what you did" lane with different marks.
  • What happens at six weeks. This view does not scale — twenty-eight lanes will not fit. The six-week scale keeps its own picture (the coverage grid), and the two are linked by C-17's ladder rather than being the same chart zoomed.
  • The unknown day. Wednesday here has nothing at all. It is drawn as a ruled but empty row, which is the same grammar as the coverage grid's unknown — worth checking that the two read as the same state.
