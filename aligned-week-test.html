One week, one axis

We had been designing each logging category its own picture. This is the other thing: every dimension on the same time axis, so the correlations are visible rather than asserted. One week only — the scale where detail is affordable.

Tutu, 2026-08-27. "It makes sense for each logging category to have its own visualization and summary, but what could be more interesting is to visualize all dimensions based on the same time axis, so the user can actually see the correlations… we are helping the user connect the dots." And: "let the chart tell the story" — fewer statements, not more.

The week

Two groupings, three scales. By day — dimensions as lanes inside each day. By measure — one panel per measure, all on a shared axis. And 1 / 3 / 7 days, which changes what a mark can afford to say and what a finger is allowed to aim at.

What the chart is doing

Feeling is the ground, not a lane

A rough patch is a period, not an event — so it is drawn as a soft span behind the whole day, crossing every other lane. That is the same figure/ground move we landed on for coverage, one level down, and it is what makes the correlation legible: you don't compare a "feeling row" against a "dose row", you see the dose sitting inside the rough patch or outside it.

Sleep is a band, anchored at its end

Sleep crosses midnight, so it belongs to the day you woke on. The band enters from the left edge and fades, meaning "this started before the window" — the duration is stated rather than measured off the axis, because measuring a clipped bar is a lie.

Add-ons are a treatment, not a second mark — and there are two states, not three

Per D-16 a slot is one ask, so it is one mark: a ring around the dose dot means the slot included the add-on, and no ring means it didn't. I had drawn a third state, a broken ring for "add-on skipped". Tutu: "I don't think we need add-on skipped. User would say with or without add-on."

The distinction underneath is worth keeping. "Skipped" is a comparison against the plan, which the app computes. "With / without" is what the person actually said. Drawing the computed one turns the chart into a compliance check — the report card wearing a third hat, and this time in a place we would not have looked for it. The plan deviation is still recoverable from the data if we ever deliberately want it; it just isn't something the picture should volunteer.

Two groupings, two questions

Tutu: "could we fit 3 or more charts on one screen, stacking style, but share the same x-axis?"

Yes, and it is a different chart rather than a tidier one. Same data, transposed:

  • By day groups by day and puts the dimensions inside it as lanes. It answers "what did Tuesday look like". The correlation is read across lanes within a row.
  • By measure groups by dimension — one panel each, seven day-rows inside, all on one shared axis. It answers "when do doses happen" and, crossing panels, "does the rough stretch always land at the same hour". The correlation is read down a column across panels.

The second is the stronger layout for finding a pattern and the weaker one for reading a single day, which is why both are here. It also keeps the figure/ground move alive through the transpose: the rough stretches ghost into every panel, so you can see a dose sitting inside one without leaving the dose panel.

The stack changes what a target is — and that is C-17 working

By measure there are twenty-eight rows of 11px on screen. No row can be a target, and that is fine, because the unit worth interrogating here isn't a day at all — it's a time of day. So the target becomes a part of the day: a full-height column, 44px wide at its narrowest on a 390pt phone, and the readout it produces is the sentence this layout was built to support — "Early afternoon — 6 doses, 5 with an add-on, 2 high-protein meals, and 2 of the 3 rough stretches begin here."

Which is the accessible / sensible / narrative test doing real work rather than confirming a choice already made. The target changed because the zoom changed; the chart never stopped responding.

Activity gets a lane, and it is sleep's lane twice

Both are intervals — something that started and ended — so both are bands, same shape, same rounding, same behaviour. In the day view only hue separates them; in the stack, where each panel is titled, even the hue goes and they are simply two bands in two places. That is the same principle as the palette note below: a difference already carried by position or by a label does not need a second carrier.

It also makes something visible that had nowhere to live before. Saturday's swim sits inside the rough stretch rather than before or after it — the kind of thing a person might want to notice, and exactly the kind of thing we must not conclude anything about.

Protein: count, don't measure — and don't imply a target

Tutu: "the one block or two blocks do not need to show the empty block, because it suggests 'this is not full, not 100%' while for meals, we don't measure that."

That overrides my own argument. I had kept empty cells because they state the scale — you can see three levels exist even on a one-block day. But an empty cell is unfilled capacity, and capacity implies a target the data does not have. It is the completion frame the quiet-day reframe threw out, arriving through geometry instead of colour, which is precisely where we would not have looked for it.

The cost is real and worth stating: without empty cells, one block no longer announces that three exist. A price worth paying to keep a meal from looking like a goal.

Vertical or horizontal — and this one has an answer

I built it horizontal; Tutu was picturing vertical. Both are in the toggle, and I think vertical wins on a point that isn't taste:

This is a time axis. Horizontal extent already means duration everywhere else on the chart — sleep bands, activity bars, rough stretches. A horizontal protein stack therefore makes a quiet false claim: that a three-protein meal lasted longer than a one-protein meal. The vertical axis inside a lane carries no meaning yet, so it is free, and it reads as quantity the way a small bar does.

The counter-argument is legibility, and it is not weak. Horizontal blocks sit side by side, so three of them are easy to count at a glance; vertical blocks have to fit inside a lane's height, and at seven days that height is about fifteen pixels for all three. In the stacked view it is worse — an 12px row leaves 3px per block. Compare them on the phone: the argument above may not survive contact with the actual size.

One day, three days, seven

Tutu: "I kind of feel there could be scale at 1 day, 3 days, and 7 days… I want to show the options to users and narrow them down."

Room is the whole difference, and it changes two things at once.

  • What a mark can say. At seven days a mark can only be a mark. At three, the lanes can carry their own names and activities can be labelled. At one day there is space for each dose to state its time and whether the add-on came with it — the chart stops needing a legend or a sentence underneath to be readable.
  • What a finger may aim at. At seven and three days the day is the target — the whole row. At one day the entries are far enough apart to be targets themselves, so they are. Same rule, finer unit, because the room exists. That is C-17's ladder stated in pixels rather than in principle.
Two questions this doesn't answer. One: three scales is three, and the six-week coverage grid is a fourth. Whether a person wants four or two is not something the page can settle — but the honest suspicion is that 1 day and 7 days are the two real ones, and 3 exists mainly because it looked like it should. Worth putting all four in front of P01 and the domain lead and letting them cut. Two: five lanes across seven days is now about 530px of chart — a screen and a half on a phone before the sentence underneath. Adding activity is what did it. Either some lanes are off by default, or the seven-day scale carries fewer measures than the one-day scale does, which would be another rung on the same ladder.

One ink, and why only in the stack

Tutu: "another option might not necessarily need different legends. They could be same or similar colours as long as the chart type makes sense, because each chart has its subtitle."

This turned out to draw a line between the two views rather than being a preference.

  • By measure, every panel carries a title and a subtitle. The title is what identifies the measure, so hue is a second, redundant encoding of something already named in words — and four hues for four labelled panels is noise. In one ink the panels differ by mark type — band, dot, blocks, span — which is a real difference, and weight carries the rest. The legend disappears entirely, because a legend here would be the same fact in a third form: hue, title and swatch.
  • By day, nothing labels the lanes. Hue is the only thing telling sleep from meals, so it can't be given up — the toggle is switched off in that view rather than offered and quietly wrong.
The general form. Colour earns its place when it is the only carrier of a distinction. The moment a label carries the same distinction, colour is free — and in a palette this quiet, spending it twice is what makes a chart feel busy without anyone being able to say which element to remove.

The statement is secondary

One quiet line under the chart, and the coverage line under that. The chart is doing the work; the sentence only names what you are already looking at.

What I can't settle here. Three things need real data or a real person. One: whether four lanes is already too many on a 320pt phone — meals is the first candidate to drop, since protein matters to timing and timing is visible in the dose lane anyway. Two: whether the rough-span ground reads as an observation or as a verdict on the day — the same trap as the report card, one level down. Three: whether people read the vertical alignment at all without being told; the whole design assumes they scan down a time column, and that is an assumption, not a finding.

Still open

  • Activity has no lane. Walks, exercise, drumming — currently they would have to live in the meal lane as generic events, which muddles it. Either a fifth lane, or events and meals merge into one "what you did" lane with different marks.
  • What happens at six weeks. This view does not scale — twenty-eight lanes will not fit. The six-week scale keeps its own picture (the coverage grid), and the two are linked by C-17's ladder rather than being the same chart zoomed.
  • The unknown day. Wednesday here has nothing at all. It is drawn as a ruled but empty row, which is the same grammar as the coverage grid's unknown — worth checking that the two read as the same state.
