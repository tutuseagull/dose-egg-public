Look Back charts, actually touched

Five forms, all live, all running off one six-week set of made-up data. Open it on a phone. Each one is here to answer a different question, and the last section is about the charts we deliberately did not build.

The rule underneath all of them. Glucose has a sensor; dopamine does not. A CGM samples every few minutes, so a continuous line is an honest rendering. DoseEgg has sparse, self-reported, irregular events, and a line drawn between two self-reports invents the gap between them. So: the mark may not claim more resolution than the data has. Sparse events are dots. Counts over a bounded period are bars. What we know versus don't know is a grid. No lines, no areas, no smoothing, no trendlines.
Why the marks are small. Two arguments. One: people drill into a data point mostly when it looks like an outlier — and an outlier is by definition the mark with space around it, so precision matters least exactly where the clutter is worst. Two: reading a chart carefully is a good-capacity activity, not something done mid-OFF. That makes data exploration a different context of use, which is what the touch-target rule already says to size against. Floor here is the WCAG 2.2 AA minimum, 24px effective — with arrow keys underneath for anyone who would rather not aim.

A — day rows

"What did my week look like?"

Each row is a day, and the dots are the times doses actually landed. The row is the target, so there is nothing small to hit. Tap any row — including an empty one.

dose you confirmed assumed
Try an empty row. A blank day is not drawn as a zero and it does not get a warning colour — the readout says "No entries. This picture doesn't cover it." The grammar is the whole trick: the subject is the record, not the person. "You missed Tuesday" makes them the failure; "this picture doesn't cover Tuesday" states the same fact with no verdict in it.
The selected day survives a week change. Pick Tuesday, step back a week, still Tuesday — with that week's times. That fell out of storing selection as an index rather than a date. It is also the domain lead's "compare two days side by side" in embryo: same weekday, different weeks, compared across time instead of across space.

B — days across, clock time up

"How steady is each dose?"

The transpose of A: each column is a day, height is the time on the clock. Drag the line, or just tap. One line reads all three doses of that day at once. Then switch to Six weeks — same question, one zoom level out.

dose you confirmed the time you planned
one confirmed dose − earlier than planned · + later
What the scale switch actually does. Zooming out here changes the mark, it does not just widen the window — which is the same move Apple's six-month sleep view makes when it stops drawing individual nights and collapses each week into a range. At week scale a day is a column you can scrub. At six-week scale the days are gathered into three clouds and you read shape: a narrow cloud is a steady slot, a tall one wanders. Different question, so a different mark.

Two details worth noticing. The horizontal spread is a beeswarm, not random jitter — dots fan out symmetrically from the centre, so the width of the cloud is density. And it is fine that x means nothing here, because nothing in this view is selectable. Jitter is only a lie when you can point at a dot and ask which day it is. That is why the old slot-columns draft had to become day columns the moment we wanted a scrub — and why it earns its place back at this scale.

One thing changed on the way: the y-axis is minutes around your planned time, not clock time. On a shared clock axis each slot's 90-minute spread collapses into a flat smear near its own hour and the three clouds cannot be compared at all. Measuring each slot against its own planned time puts all three on one scale, and the question — how wide is each slot — becomes the literal height of the cloud. This is the one chart here where distance from a line is the message, so it must stay descriptive: earlier and later, never early and late, and never a colour that sorts them.

Assumed doses are left out of the clouds. A schedule is a plan, not an observation, so it cannot appear in a claim about timing — and the readout says how many were excluded rather than quietly dropping them.
The dashed rails are the times you planned — not your average. That distinction is the whole rule. A reference line is legitimate only if somebody chose it. A planned dose time is a decision you made with your doctor, so missing it is information and it points somewhere useful: the schedule. An average of your own dose times is emergent, not chosen — it is made out of the very dots you are looking at, so "distance from average" is just the spread restated, with a line added that reads like a target nobody set. And a shaded "on target" band is worse still: it sorts your days into pass and fail.

Chosen → a line. Emergent → no line. Imposed by a population → never [C-10]. Try the toggle: without the rails you can still see spread, but you cannot see drift. That is what the rails buy, and it is why the statement "your evening dose lands close to when you planned it" needs them as evidence.
What the scrub forced. A vertical line only means something if the x-axis means something. In the first draft of B, horizontal position was pure jitter — spacing to stop dots overlapping — so a scrub line would have selected nothing. Wanting the interaction is what turned x into the day. The interaction improved the chart, which is the opposite of the usual order.

And one line held: a drag may never write anything. Scrubbing is exploratory and reversible, so a missed target costs nothing — unlike the scroll-wheel time picker, where the gesture landed a value on the record. Gesture cost scales with consequence: drag to read, tap to write.

The alignment pair

"Is my afternoon dip about the clock, or about the dose?"

The same eight notes, plotted two ways. Tap between them.

This is the one place a chart beats a sentence. Tight on the left and scattered on the right means the dip tracks the clock, not the medication. Circadian means the body's own 24-hour rhythm — things that land at the same hour whatever you did. Pharmacokinetic (PK) means what the body does to a drug over time, so a PK dip arrives a fixed interval after a dose. Patients call that wearing-off. They feel identical and take opposite responses: one is a dose-spacing conversation, the other is a sleep and light conversation. No sentence separates them. Two frames of the same eight dots do it instantly.
The app shows both and names neither cause. Naming it would be a diagnosis. And there is an irony worth knowing: this test only computes if the person's dose times actually vary — with a perfectly steady schedule both frames look the same, so the most disciplined logger is the one it can never serve. The tempting fix, nudging someone to vary a dose so the contrast appears, is banned outright. Prescribing to improve our own data is worse than prescribing.

C — coverage grid

"How much of this can the record actually speak for?"

Six weeks, one square per day. Filled means there are entries; dashed means we know nothing. Tap any square.

days with entries nothing recorded
This is the honest version of the contribution graph. It is one design decision away from a GitHub-style streak grid, which is a machine for making people feel bad about gaps. Three things keep it on the right side: the empty state is a dashed outline (unknown) and not a pale fill (zero); there is no total, no longest-run, no percentage; and the caption talks about the record, never about the person. Remove any one of those and it becomes the thing we banned.

It is also the only chart here that shows an ethical position rather than a measurement: absence of a log is unknown, never zero.

D — count bars

"How much movement did I actually do?"

Six weeks of logged movement sessions — walks, PT, boxing, tai chi. Tap a bar and the two charts above jump to that week.

Counting a therapy is allowed. Counting app usage is not. Exercise is the strongest non-drug intervention in Parkinson's, so a count of it is a count of treatment delivered — something a doctor asks about. A count of days logged would be neither: it praises using the product, manufactures a streak, and punishes exactly the weeks someone was too unwell to log. Count the therapy, never count the app.

Note what is missing: no goal line, no target, no "up from last week." A goal line turns every short bar into a failure, and this is precisely the number a person on a bad month cannot control.
This is also the only chart here that lives above the week. A, B and the coverage grid answer questions inside a period; this one answers a question about periods. Which makes it a natural period picker — tapping a bar moves the other charts — rather than a separate destination.

What is deliberately not here

  • Any line chart. Self-reported events are not a continuous signal. A line between two entries is a claim about the hours in between that nobody measured.
  • Trendlines, arrows, deltas and percentage changes. Apple's own six-month sleep view carries none of them — a company with a full sensor stack and continuous data still declines to tell you your sleep is getting worse. For a progressive condition the bar is higher, not lower.
  • Red and green. A colour scale that runs from bad to good turns a record into a grade. One hue plus a neutral, everywhere.
  • A sleep-stage style band chart for ON/OFF state. That shape is right, and it is reserved — for the day a wearable actually supplies continuous state. Drawing it from self-report would claim a resolution we do not have.
  • Averages, benchmarks, and "patients like you." The only meaningful baseline is the person's own history.
