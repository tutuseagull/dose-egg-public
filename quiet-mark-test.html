Drawing a quiet day

Five ways to render three states — logged, quiet, unknown — in the three places they have to survive. Open on a phone. Tap an option to swap all three charts at once.

The problem, in Tutu's words. A quiet day is a real, valid record — someone was there and said the day was ordinary. But an empty circle reads as missing, and missing is what unknown already means. Two different facts, both drawn as absence.

The reframe

The axis isn't eventful vs uneventful. It's spoken vs unspoken.

Our current grammar accidentally encodes "how much happened," which slides toward "how bad was it." The honest axis is how much the record can speak for — and on that axis a quiet day is fully spoken. It should be as visually solid as a logged one.

Which gives the rule, and it's rule 5 from the portfolio list applied one level down: presence encodes testimony, content encodes what happened. Someone spoke, so the mark exists. What they said is a property of the mark, not of whether it's drawn.

The trap on the other side. If quiet gets drawn as more than logged — brighter, warmer, a check — we've built the report card. It should be equal in weight and different in kind. Neither reward nor deficit.

The five

Each option decides how the three states differ. Watch what happens under low contrast — that's where most of them die.

1 · Coverage grid — six weeks, one square per day

The hardest context. Forty-two marks read at a glance. Auto fills the width, which is how it should ship; the fixed sizes are for comparing.

2 · Day rows — one week, time of day across

Here a quiet day is a whole empty row. The row itself has to say something.

3 · Timeline — three consecutive days

Scrolling back through the record. A quiet day still needs to occupy space.

4 · The same six weeks, with its sentence

The marks and the statements were made on different days and had never met. This is them together, on the grid above.

What this option says

Keypad or chart?

Two rounds. Tutu, on her phone: "auto is really big — and now they feel each container could be interactive because they are so big." Then, on the fix: "what do you mean by pressable? I feel individual day could be pressable? did I misunderstand?"

She didn't. I did. I read "these feel interactive" as "this affordance is false", and reached for our navigation-vs-inspection rule to justify it. That rule says what a selection means at each scale. It never said the six-week grid has no selection. Recording the wrong turn because the right answer is only interesting next to it.

A day has to be tappable. Otherwise the only route from "Jul 27 looks rough" to "what happened on Jul 27" is scrolling a timeline back six weeks — the worst possible ask for this population, and it would make the picture a dead end.

So the problem was never a false affordance. It's that 42 equal-weight filled rectangles read as a keypad, and a keypad says operate me where a chart says read me.

The grid's first job is to be read at a glance — the shape of six weeks, in one look. Tapping is real but secondary, and it shouldn't be shouting over the picture. Which turns the touch-target worry from an objection into a requirement:

The target is not the ink. The whole day slot is the hit area — about 43px at auto, the same in every container and at every cell size — while the drawn mark stays a dot. A target is a promise about the finger; the mark is a statement about the day. They are allowed to differ, and here they should.

Three separable causes

A tinted, rounded rectangle with a gutter around it is a keypad key. But that's three decisions, and they come apart — which is what the toggle above tests.

  • Size. Auto caps at 38px rather than 46. Cheapest part of the fix.
  • Styling — radius and fill contrast. Flat squares the corners and drops to the paler ground.
  • Discreteness — 42 separate objects. Field merges each run of covered days into one shape; day boundaries become hairline seams instead of gutters.

Field, and why it lost

Field was where I'd landed: merge each run of covered days into one shape, day boundaries become hairline seams, forty-two objects become five. It is decisively the least keypad-like of the three, and it looked like the option that finally drew what "coverage is the canvas" always claimed.

Tutu: "field feels more like a continuous relationship... it's just the same hour of different days."

Which kills it. A fused band asserts a continuity the data doesn't have. Each cell is a whole day — a sample, not a segment. There is no value between Monday and Tuesday to read, so drawing them fused invites an interpolation the record can't support.

And the second-order cost is worse than the first. Once days fuse, run length becomes a visual channel — and it carries no meaning except streak. A long unbroken band reads as good, a fragmented one as bad. That is precisely the report card the reframe threw out: "if quiet gets drawn as more than logged, we've built the report card." Field builds it back with geometry instead of colour, which is harder to notice and just as loud.

Where this lands: tight

Days stay discrete, so nothing false is asserted and no length means anything. The radius stays — Tutu: "I like the rounded corner, I don't like flat, looks sharp" — and squaring it had bought very little anyway. The keypad read came mostly from the tint and the gutter, and C-17 took the rest of it away by moving the target off the cell entirely. So the only differences from tiles are the paler ground and a gutter tightened from 22% to 12%: days still part, because they are separate samples and should look it, but the row coheres instead of scattering into floating keys.

Selection is feedback, not the target

Tutu: "I don't know if it's necessary to show the row. We can just show the selected tile."

Right, and it's the same conflation a third time — I drew the target. The row is what the finger claims, and that stays; but a ring around it shows the user the hit area, which is apparatus. Targets should be invisible. Feedback belongs on the thing that matters. Three treatments to compare on the phone:

  • Tile — the selected day inverts: the ground takes the mark colour and the mark goes to paper. No ring, no box. An unknown day keeps its dashed edge, in paper, so inverting never costs the state.
  • Crosshair — the same, plus everything off the selected row and column recedes to 22%. Not decoration: the column is a real axis here — the same weekday across six weeks — so the fade answers "how are my Fridays" for free, which is exactly the kind of pattern the retrospective exists to surface.
  • Row ring — the version this replaces, kept so the difference is visible rather than argued.
Worth watching for. The inverted tile is the highest-contrast object on the screen, which is right for feedback and wrong if it ever persists — a selection that stays after you look away becomes a fourth state competing with the three real ones. It should clear on navigation.

Where I'd land

Option 4 — ground and figure, with a piece of option 3 borrowed.

Coverage becomes a property of the canvas, not of the mark. A day the record covers gets a tinted ground; a day it doesn't gets no ground and a dashed edge. Events are marks drawn on that ground. Then quiet needs no special mark at all — it's simply a covered day with nothing on it, which is exactly what it means.

That has three properties the others don't:

  • It never competes with the data. Coverage sits behind, events sit in front. Adding a fourth state later doesn't cost us a fourth mark shape.
  • It survives low contrast, because a filled area is far more robust to contrast loss than a 1.5px outline or a weight difference between two small dots.
  • It says the right sentence. "This part of the picture is covered" is a statement about the record. A hollow dot is a statement about the day.

The borrowed piece: in the grid specifically, where cells are tiny and there's no figure/ground room, a solid short tick for quiet is more legible than an untouched tinted cell. Different shape beats different weight at 20px.

The one I'd argue against is option 5. Making unknown nearly invisible is tempting — it's the least informative state, so give it the least ink. But a gap you can't see is a lie by omission, and the whole point of the coverage grid is that the holes are visible. Unknown has to stay legible even though it's saying nothing.

Still open

  • Does a quiet day need a time? An entry has a timestamp. "Today was ordinary" is a claim about a whole day, or an afternoon. If it's day-scoped it's a different object from every other entry, which the schema has to know.
  • Can a day be both? Quiet morning, rough afternoon. If quiet is day-scoped, no. If it's period-scoped, the grid cell needs to hold a mixed state and we're back to four marks.
  • Retroactive quiet. Marking last Tuesday ordinary from memory is weaker evidence than marking today ordinary. Same problem as an assumed dose — and we already solved that one with a distinct mark, so there's precedent either way.
  • The word. "Quiet" is our internal name. Test it: quiet may read as lonely. Candidates to try on P01 and the domain lead — an ordinary day · nothing to report · a steady day · uneventful. Per the OFF-period vocabulary research, offer more than one and let people keep their own.
