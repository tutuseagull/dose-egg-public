The rules any option has to keep

  • Never move what someone is about to tap. A target that slides while a hand with tremor is on its way is a missed tap. Motion happens to what changed, after the tap.
  • Never make anyone wait. Every tap counts the moment it lands, mid-animation included; motion only shows what already happened.
  • No overshoot. A bounce reads as playful, and for a vestibular-sensitive eye, as wobble. Our spring settles without passing its mark.
  • Short. 120 to 320 ms, the range the token page already has. The press is 90 ms in, so the release is felt.
  • Reduce motion keeps the feedback, drops the movement. State still changes, by a short fade. Parkinson's medication can bring dizziness; this setting is not an edge case here.
  • Motion means something. The one place we would spend the showier option is where the movement IS the message: an assumed dose becoming a confirmed one.

What the field does

  • Carbon (IBM) splits motion into productive (quick, stays out of the way, for task work) and expressive (reserved for significant moments), with durations from 70 to 700 ms. DoseEgg is a productive app with one expressive moment.
  • Material 3 Expressive (Google, 2025) moved from fixed curves to springs, with a standard scheme (settles) and an expressive one (bounces). We take the standard spring.
  • Apple: Liquid Glass elements morph and merge rather than cut, which is Option A for tabs. iOS 26.4 added "Reduce Bright Effects" and a stronger Reduce Motion after people found the glass animations tiring, a reminder that delight has a cost for sensitive eyes.
  • The web can now animate between pages (cross-document View Transitions, in Safari 18.2 and Chrome 126 onward). Today and Look Back are separate pages, and the pill can still glide between them. Older browsers simply switch, with nothing broken.
