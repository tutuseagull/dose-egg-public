Look Back — statement bank

The sentences a patient would open the app to read, written before any screen. Each one carries what it needs to be true, what it may be drawn as, and what may never be drawn on it.

Draft 2 · August 2026 · for P01 and the domain lead to react to. Not a spec.

The method here is deliberate. The failure mode in an analytics surface is building a chart and then hunting for a story in it. So this page is written in the other order: the statement first, the visual second, and for several statements the honest answer is no visual at all.

How to read this page. Everything is a proposal. The most useful thing a reader can do is mark statements as would open the app for this / don't care / this would upset me. The third category is the most valuable of the three.

Two readers, two scenarios

The same surface has two plausible audiences, and they want opposite things. Naming both keeps us from designing a compromise that serves neither.

Scenario 1 — the 8am reader

Opens the app over coffee. Wants to know what should I expect today, so they can decide whether to schedule the meeting, take the trip, book the class.

We cannot serve this honestly. Research is explicit that ON/OFF periods are plannable but not predictable [C-03]; a confident morning forecast would be manufactured confidence, and a wrong one costs the user a day. Everything we could truthfully say to this reader is a description of the past, which is Scenario 2's content arriving at the wrong hour.

Scenario 2 — the night-before-the-appointment reader

Opens the app the evening before a neurology visit, or the weekend before. Wants to know what do I tell them — and underneath that, do I have the standing to ask for a change.

This we can serve completely and honestly, because it is entirely retrospective. It is also where the interval is naturally long enough for the data to say something.

Where this leaves MVP. Build the retrospective surface first, as a neutral, non-judgmental way to see your own record. The pre-appointment framing is the destination, not the first build — designing the visit-prep flow is a later conversation and carries a research dependency on doctors that the retrospective view does not.
Parked, deliberately. Reading the user's calendar to connect planning with doses and meals — plausible and interesting, but it edges toward telling someone how to arrange their day, which is medical suggestion. Revisit with the ambient-AI consent bar [C-14], not before.

Rules every statement obeys

1 · Every claim carries its coverage. A statement names how many days it is built from and how many it could not see. "Six of nine" is a fact; "six" is a rhetorical trick.
2 · Absence of a log is unknown, never zero. A day with no entry is not a day with no symptoms. It stays a gap in the picture, and it never renders as an empty bar at the baseline. [AGENTS.md · Logging & data model]
3 · A bare entry still counts in the denominator. Depth is optional, identity is not — an entry without context is a full member of the population, it simply cannot serve as evidence for a context claim. Excluding bare entries would compute every pattern over only the days someone felt well enough to elaborate.
4 · Assumed doses are excluded from timing claims. A scheduled time is a plan, not an observation. Timing statements are built from asserted entries only, and the count of excluded assumptions is part of the coverage. [AGENTS.md · basis]
5 · Never emit a pattern whose obvious action is to do less of something. The same correlation can be aimed at the medication schedule or at the person's life. Always aim it at the schedule.
6 · No locked or greyed-out insight, ever. When there isn't enough yet, say something shorter and true, or say when to come back. Never show the shape of a reward that has been withheld.
7 · The mark may not claim more resolution than the data has. See the visual language section below.

Visual language — four options

Glucose has a sensor. A CGM samples every few minutes, so a continuous line is an honest rendering — the data really is continuous. DoseEgg has sparse, self-reported, irregular events. A line drawn between two self-reports invents the gap between them and implies a trajectory nobody measured.

So: no line charts, no areas, no smoothing, no trendlines. Sparse events are dots. Counts over a bounded period are bars. What we know versus don't know is a grid. The mark must not claim more resolution than the data has.
A reference line is legitimate only if somebody chose it. The dashed rails are the times you planned — a decision made with a doctor. Missing one is information, and it points at the schedule, which is where we want it pointed. An average of your own dose times is emergent rather than chosen: it is made out of the very dots you are looking at, so "distance from average" restates the spread while adding a line that reads like a target nobody set. A shaded "on target" band is worse still — it sorts days into pass and fail. And a population norm is banned outright [C-10].

Chosen → a line. Emergent → no line. Imposed → never. The rails also matter because statement R-02 ("your evening dose lands close to when you planned it") has no evidence without them — hide them and you can still read spread, but you cannot read drift.
Slot columns earned its way back — as a scale, not a separate chart. The day-column form and the slot-cloud form are now two zoom levels of the same card, switched like Apple's D/W/M/6M: This week shows seven day columns you can scrub; Six weeks gathers every confirmed dose into three clouds you read as shapes. Zooming out changes the mark, it does not just widen the window. And the jitter that was fatal at week scale is fine here, because nothing in this view is selectable — jitter is only a lie when you can point at a dot and ask which day it is.
The long view had to change its y-axis to work. On a shared clock axis each slot's 90-minute spread collapses into a flat smear beside its own hour, and the three clouds cannot be compared at all. Plotting minutes around that slot's planned time puts all three on one scale, and "how wide is each slot" becomes the literal height of the cloud. It is also the one chart where a dot's distance from a line is the message — so the labels stay descriptive (earlier and later, never early and late) and no colour ever sorts them. Two further consequences: the spread is a beeswarm, so cloud width reads as density rather than noise; and assumed doses are excluded with the count disclosed, because a schedule is a plan, not an observation [rule 4].
A legend lists exactly what is drawn — and explains, rather than names. Caught in review: the week view rendered hollow assumed marks but the legend only listed confirmed doses and the planned rails, so the one mark a reader could not guess was the one left unexplained. Two halves to the rule. Completeness: if a mark can appear in the chart it must appear in the legend, and if it cannot appear it must not — the six-week view has no assumed swatch precisely because assumed doses are excluded there. Explanation: "assumed" is a label a reader has to decode; "assumed — from the schedule, not confirmed" is the fact itself. In a product where the difference between an observation and an assumption carries the whole honesty argument, that phrase is not legend chrome, it is the argument in miniature.
A gridline is never neutral. Drawing the planned rails and plain clock gridlines put a dashed line five pixels from a solid one — visually noisy, and it obscured the real point. So in the live version the horizontal reference swaps: either the times you planned, or plain clock hours, never both. Toggling between them is the clearest demonstration on the whole page that a chart's background is a claim about what to measure against.

The one place a chart beats a sentence

C-07 requires that a circadian dip (same clock time every day) be distinguishable from a dose-cycle dip (same interval after a dose). Those two causes need completely different responses — one is a medication conversation, the other is a sleep and light conversation — and no single sentence separates them. Two renderings of the same five marks do it instantly.

Interaction — reading a chart with a tremor

These are inline SVG, so every mark is a real element in the page and attaching a tap handler is a few lines. The interesting question was never the code — it was whether marks below the 48px routine tier are acceptable here at all. Settled: yes, because a chart is a different context of use, which is what the touch-target rule already says to size against [AGENTS.md]. Three reasons, all worth keeping:

  • Drill-down targets outliers — and an outlier is by definition the mark with space around it. Precision matters least exactly where the clutter is worst.
  • A near-miss inside a cluster is forgiving, because the neighbour it lands on is nearly the same value. The cost of a mis-tap scales with how far apart the options are.
  • Reading a chart closely is a good-capacity activity, not something done mid-OFF. It takes cognitive room as well as motor control, so the crisis tier does not apply.
Floor in this context: the WCAG 2.2 AA minimum, 24px effective — with a no-aim path always present (stepper buttons, arrow keys). The floor was never about the size of the ink; it is about the size of the region that responds. A 5px dot inside a 40×170px invisible band is a bigger target than most buttons.
Scrub is fine here — I was wrong to rule it out. Dragging a vertical line converts a two-dimensional precision task into a one-dimensional one and hands over the full height of the chart as target area, which is more forgiving than poking a dot. The scroll-wheel time picker was a bad analogy: that gesture landed a value on the record, so a slip had a consequence. A scrub is exploratory and reversible, and a slip costs nothing. The rule that survives: gesture cost scales with consequence — drag to read, tap to write. A drag may never write to the record.
What the scrub forced. A vertical line only means something if the x-axis means something. In the first draft of Option B, horizontal position was pure jitter — spacing to stop dots overlapping — so a scrub line would have selected nothing. Wanting the interaction is what turned x into the day. The interaction improved the chart, which is the opposite of the usual order. It also bought something unplanned: one line now reads morning, midday and evening at once, which is the comparison a person actually wants — not "what time was Thursday's midday dose" but "what did Thursday look like."
Selection and navigation are different jobs and must not look the same. Selection moves inside the view — which day am I reading. Navigation moves the view — which week am I reading. A first draft gave each chart "‹ Earlier day / Later day ›" buttons and they were misread as week navigation, because a pair of arrows under a chart is conventionally a period control. Worse, they pointed sideways while Option A's selection moves down the rows — the control contradicted the motion it caused. Arrows mean weeks. Selection is touch and arrow keys only. And Clear belongs beside the thing it clears — in the readout, next to the day and times, not orphaned in a button row.
Period context goes above the chart, and the awkward control has a cheap fix. Reading order is period → value → plot, so the week sits on top. What had pushed it below was "Jump to this week" — a control that only exists once you've navigated away, so it either shifts the layout or squats on space it hasn't earned. Two moves solve it. The centre of the nav is one fixed-height block whose second line is a status ("This week") or an action ("↩ Jump to this week"): same footprint either way, and when it's an action the whole block is the target — ~212×52px, not a line of text. And the readout is never empty — with nothing selected it describes the week ("Whole week · 21 doses on the record · 1 assumed") instead of prompting you to tap. Neither row ever changes height, so the chart never moves.

The general form of that fix is worth keeping: when a control only exists in one state, give the slot a job in the other state rather than letting it collapse. A status line and an action can share a footprint. An empty state and a summary can share one too — and the summary is more useful than the prompt it replaces.

An accident worth keeping. Because selection is stored as a day index rather than a date, stepping to another week holds the same weekday — pick Tuesday, go back a week, still Tuesday with that week's times. That is the two-day comparison the domain lead raised, in embryo: same weekday, different weeks, compared across time rather than across space. Cheap, already working, and worth showing her.

All four forms plus the alignment pair are built and touchable in chart-interaction-test.html — open it on a phone. Same house as touch-target-test.html: the way to settle an argument about targets is to tap them. Note what stays true across A and B: A reads the shape of a day, B reads steadiness across days. Linked views of one dataset, not competing options.

One dataset behind all five. The test page runs every chart off a single six-week array in which a day is either a list of doses or null. That one null is what makes the empty rows in A, the gaps in the coverage grid, and the "this picture doesn't cover it" copy agree with each other without being maintained separately — the honesty rule is enforced by the data model rather than remembered by whoever draws the next chart. Worth carrying into the build: if "unknown" is a value in the schema, no chart can accidentally render it as a zero.
The count bars are the only form that lives above the week. A, B and the coverage grid answer questions inside a period; counts answer a question about periods. Which makes the bar chart a natural period picker — tapping a bar moves the other charts — rather than a separate destination. Built that way in the test page.

Granularity — the D / W / M / 6M question

Apple's period tabs are a zoom control over continuous sensor data. Our three eras are a statement-type progression. They look similar and are not the same thing, and conflating them would be confusing — a longer window doesn't unlock a different kind of sentence, having more data does.

Post-MVP, agreed — but the reason is not only that there won't be enough data yet:

Zooming out must change the mark, not shrink it. Apple's own 6M sleep view is the demonstration: it stops drawing individual nights and collapses each week into a single earliest-to-latest range bar. If Look Back ever gets a 6-month view, the dose-time dot strip becomes a weekly range band — not 180 unreadable dots.

Two more things worth taking from those screens. Apple's 6M view carries no trendline, no arrow, no percentage change — a company with a full sensor stack and six months of continuous data still declines to tell you your sleep is getting worse. That is independent support for the annotation rule. And the daily hypnogram — the stacked Awake / REM / Core / Deep bands — is the shape we would want for continuous ON/OFF state, if a wearable ever supplies it. It is on the list of forms to reserve, not to imitate: drawing that shape from self-report would claim a resolution we don't have [rule 7].

The coverage control

Proposed by Tutu: let the user decide whether days with no entry count in the denominator, since only they know whether a blank day was a hard day or an unbothered one. Good instinct — it makes an assumption visible that every other health app makes silently. Three refinements before it gets designed.

1 · It only affects rate statements. "You noted 4 rough afternoons" — out of the 21 days on the calendar, or the 15 you logged? That is a real fork and the control belongs there. It does not affect co-occurrence statements: a day with no entry is neither an X day nor a not-X day, so it sits outside that comparison entirely. Building the control globally would imply it changes numbers it cannot change.
2 · Not a toggle in the chrome — a property of the statement. Persistent controls in the header ask the user to think like a statistician before they've read anything. Instead let the coverage line itself be the control: "4 rough afternoons, across the 15 days you logged · 6 days have no entry" — tapping the second half recomputes. Contextual, discoverable, invisible where it wouldn't change the number.
3 · The tooltip describes the app's limit, not the user's motive. "Some people don't log on their hardest days" is true of a population and an accusation when said to one person. Write the uncertainty as ours: "A day with no entry could be a quiet day or just an unlogged one — we can't tell from here. You can decide how they're counted." Same information, no verdict. [see the R-05 craft note]

On learning the default from usage: which setting people choose is behaviour, not content, so it sits inside the first-party analytics rule [docs/storage-and-privacy.md §4]. Worth measuring. One guard — use it to pick a better global default in a later release, never to silently personalise. A number that changes because the app quietly re-decided how to count is the same failure mode as tone that adapts to outcome: the interface starts holding an opinion it never states.

Why C-05 matters more than it looks. Almost no consumer health app will tell you it found nothing, because a null result reads as the product failing. But for a progressive condition, "this month looks like last month" is often the news the person most wants — and an app that can say it is an app you can believe when it says something did change. Shipping the null result is what makes the non-null results credible.

E-04 is the rule-5 test case. It is the same statistical shape as "your dose runs short on days you walk before lunch" — but that one, however hedged, will be heard as walk less, and exercise is the strongest non-drug intervention there is. E-04 points the same evidence somewhere it costs nothing to act on. When a co-occurrence can be stated in either direction, state the one whose obvious action is to do more, or to ask a question — never to withdraw.

What we say below the threshold

Never a locked card, never a greyed-out preview, never a progress bar toward an insight. Two moves only: say something shorter and true, or name when to come back.

T-01 · shorter and true

"So far this is a picture of four days."

Paired with
The week-in-progress chart, partially filled — the same Option A that will be complete on Sunday, not a placeholder
Never
"3 more days until your first insight."
T-02 · come back later

"Timing patterns start to show up after a couple of weeks. There's not much to say yet."

Tone target
A colleague saying "too early to tell" — not a system withholding a reward
Never
A countdown, a percentage, a lock icon, a blurred preview.
T-03 · depth invitation

"You've been logging doses. If you ever want to see what your afternoons have to do with the rest of your day, adding what you were up to is what makes that visible — a walk, PT, gardening, table tennis, choir, drums, a nap in the sun."

Fires when
≥2 weeks of dose entries with <20% carrying any activity or context
Frequency cap
Once. Not once a week — once. Then never again unless the user asks.
Why the examples matter
A clinical list ("exercise, meals, sleep") reads as homework. A human list gives permission for the small and the odd, which is where the real signal is.
Never
"Log more to unlock insights." Any framing where the app is owed something.
Open — the targeted question. A stronger version of T-03 exists: instead of a general invitation, ask for one specific missing fact on the day where knowing it would change the most — "was the 14th a walk day?" This is uncertainty sampling from active learning, applied to consumer UX: query the single highest-information missing label rather than asking for everything. It is a real idea and probably a good one, but it needs a full user story before it can be designed — who asks, when, how the user says "I don't remember," what happens to the answer, and what the hard cap is. Uncapped it becomes 鸡肋 within a fortnight. Not scoped here.

Open questions this page does not settle

  • The interval anchor. Does the period run from the last appointment, or is it a rolling 30/90 days? The appointment anchor is more meaningful and requires the user to enter a date we do not currently ask for.
  • How many statements at once. Three feels right; more starts to read as a dashboard. Untested.
  • Ordering. By recency, by confidence, or by how much the user has engaged with that kind of statement before? The third is adaptive and needs care — an adaptive surface can leak a judgment it never states.
  • Dismissal. If a statement can find the user on the Timeline, the user must be able to send it away. What "away" means — this one, this kind, forever — is unresolved.
  • Two days side by side. Raised by the domain lead, not yet specified. Possibly the strongest idea here: it needs exactly two days and the user supplies the hypothesis, so it works in week one. Tracked in TASKS.md.
  • Where the coverage control lives at the screen level, and whether tapping a coverage line is discoverable enough without a persistent affordance.
Decided. The surface is called Look Back (Aug 2026). "Analyze" promises inference the first month cannot deliver; "Review" collides with a meaning the product already has, since past days are editable and "review" reads as check and correct your entries.
