PD — Clinical terms
Asymmetric Motor Fluctuation
非对称性故障
fēi duìchèn xìng gùzhàng

When the medication effect is uneven across the body — one region is in ON state (medication working, movement fluid) while another remains in OFF state (stiff, slow, or frozen). A common example: upper body is ON while lower body remains OFF.

Relevant to log design: this can't be captured by a single ON/OFF toggle. The logging model needs to allow body-region specificity, or at minimum a free-form note. Also relevant to sensor design — gait sensors alone won't capture this if the patient is sitting with tremor-free hands.

Clinical Log design
ON / OFF State
开期 / 关期
kāi qī / guān qī

The core fluctuation pattern in PD. ON period: levodopa is working, movement is relatively normal. OFF period: medication has worn off or hasn't kicked in, resulting in stiffness, slowness, tremor, or freezing. Patients cycle between these states as medication is metabolised.

The central thing DoseEgg is trying to help users understand and track. Timing of ON/OFF transitions relative to medication doses, meals, and activity is the most clinically useful data a patient can self-report.

Clinical Core log type
Dyskinesia
异动症
yì dòng zhèng

Involuntary, uncontrolled movements — often writhing, twisting, or jerking — caused by long-term levodopa use. Unlike tremor (which occurs in OFF state), dyskinesia typically happens when medication levels are at their peak (ON state). Can be as disabling as the OFF state itself.

Important design nuance: a patient logging "movement problems" could mean tremor (OFF) or dyskinesia (peak ON) — two opposite medication states. The log must distinguish these, or clinical usefulness suffers.

Clinical Log design
Bradykinesia
运动迟缓
yùndòng chíhuǎn

Slowness of movement — one of the three cardinal signs of PD alongside tremor and rigidity. In daily life: slower walking, difficulty initiating movement, reduced facial expression, smaller handwriting (micrographia). Worsens in OFF state.

The signal most reliably captured by wrist accelerometers and gait sensors. Also detectable passively through phone typing rhythm (neuroQWERTY research). Useful to understand when designing what "passive monitoring" is actually measuring.

Clinical Sensor-relevant
Freezing of Gait
冻结步态
dòngjiē bùtài

A sudden, transient inability to initiate or continue walking — patients describe it as their feet being "glued to the floor." Usually occurs in OFF state, at doorways, when turning, or under stress. Can last seconds to minutes and significantly increases fall risk.

Difficult to capture passively — accelerometers see no movement, which is indistinguishable from standing still by choice. Likely requires self-report or a prompted check-in. High priority to log because of safety implications.

Clinical Log design
Circadian Resynchronization
昼夜节律重组
zhòuyè jiélǜ chóngzǔ

The process of realigning the body's internal 24-hour biological clock, which governs sleep-wake cycles, hormone release, and metabolism. In PD, circadian disruption is common — patients often experience altered sleep architecture, daytime sleepiness, and REM sleep behavior disorder. Resynchronization strategies include light therapy, melatonin timing, and structured medication schedules.

Relevant to DoseEgg because sleep quality is a meaningful PD tracking signal — and the timing of circadian disruption can explain symptom patterns that look like medication failure. A YOPD patient observed a consistent 1–3pm functional dip regardless of what they ate for lunch — suggesting a circadian trough rather than a pharmacokinetic problem. The two causes require completely different interventions, and time-stamped logging is the only way to separate them.

Clinical Sleep-relevant
Target
靶点
bǎ diǎn

In pharmacology, a specific molecular structure — a protein, receptor, or enzyme — that a drug is designed to interact with to produce a therapeutic effect. In PD, key targets include dopamine receptors (D1, D2), the enzyme MAO-B (inhibited by selegiline and rasagiline), and more recently, alpha-synuclein aggregation as a target for disease-modifying therapies.

Helps explain why different medications work differently for different patients, and why the combination and timing of drugs matters clinically. Useful background when designing the medication log — what's being tracked isn't just "did they take a pill" but which receptor pathway is being modulated and when.

Clinical Pharmacology
Pharmacokinetics (PK) · Wearing-off
药代动力学 · 剂末现象
yào dài dònglì xué · jì mò xiànxiàng

Pharmacokinetics — abbreviated PK in clinical and pharma writing, and the form you will meet most often in papers — is what the body does to a drug over time — absorb, peak, decline, clear. (Pharmacodynamics is the reverse: what the drug does to the body.) Wearing-off is the patient-facing name for the end of that curve in PD: levodopa levels fall and symptoms return before the next dose is due. A wearing-off dip arrives at a fixed interval after a dose — say four hours later — wherever that happens to land on the clock.

The distinction that matters for DoseEgg is clock-aligned vs dose-aligned. A circadian dip (see Circadian Resynchronization above) lands at the same time of day whatever you did; a pharmacokinetic dip lands a fixed interval after the dose. They feel identical and take opposite responses — one is a dose-spacing or formulation conversation, the other is a sleep and light conversation, and treating the wrong one adds side effects without fixing anything. Plotting the same marks twice, once by clock time and once by time-since-dose, separates them instantly [C-07]. Caveat worth remembering: the test only computes if the person's dose times actually vary — with a perfectly consistent schedule both alignments look the same, so the most disciplined logger is the one it cannot serve. The app must never suggest varying a dose to create the contrast.

Medication Titration
滴定
dī dìng

The process of gradually adjusting a medication's dose — typically starting low and increasing slowly — to find the minimum effective amount that controls symptoms while keeping side effects tolerable. In PD, levodopa is titrated carefully over weeks or months because both under-dosing (poor symptom control) and over-dosing (dyskinesia, hallucinations) carry significant consequences.

Relevant to DoseEgg's log design: patients in an active titration phase are generating especially high-value data — small dose changes have observable effects on ON/OFF windows, sleep, and energy. The timeline view should make dose changes visible as events, so a patient (or doctor) can see how function shifted before and after an adjustment. Titration is also when logging motivation is highest — worth designing the onboarding arc around this moment.

PD — Exercise & rehab
Exercise · Physical Therapy (PT) · Rehab
运动 · 物理治疗 · 康复
yùndòng · wùlǐ zhìliáo · kāngfù

The umbrella terms for structured physical activity in PD. Exercise is the broadest and the safest default. Physical therapy (PT) means clinician-led sessions, usually prescribed. Rehab is the programme-level word. Exercise is the strongest evidence-backed non-drug intervention in Parkinson's — it is a treatment, not a lifestyle extra.

Terminology decision (Aug 2026): "recovery exercise" is the wrong phrase and should not appear in UI copy. "Recovery" implies returning to a previous baseline, which a progressive condition does not do — it sets up a promise the product cannot keep and the user will feel the gap. Use exercise, PT, or the softest single-word umbrella, movement. Note the asymmetry with Chinese: 康复 is standard and unloaded in clinical Mandarin, so this constraint is English-specific — relevant if bilingual support returns post-MVP. Counting these sessions is legitimate in Analyze because it counts a therapy delivered, never app usage.

Clinical Copy rule
LSVT BIG
大幅度运动训练
dà fúdù yùndòng xùnliàn

A standardised PD physical therapy protocol built around deliberately oversized movements. PD shrinks movement amplitude without the person noticing (the same mechanism behind micrographia — small handwriting), so the training retrains the internal sense of "normal size" by practising exaggerated reaching, stepping, and turning. Typically delivered as an intensive block of sessions with a certified therapist.

Relevant as a named log type: a patient in an LSVT BIG block is doing something structurally different from "a walk," and the distinction is one a doctor would want to see. Also a titration-adjacent moment — function can change noticeably during a block, which makes the surrounding weeks unusually informative.

Clinical Log design
LSVT LOUD
大声训练
dàshēng xùnliàn

The speech-therapy counterpart to LSVT BIG. PD reduces vocal loudness and the person's own perception of it — they believe they are speaking normally while others cannot hear them (hypophonia). The protocol trains sustained loud phonation to recalibrate that perception.

A reminder that PD is not only motor. Voice, swallowing, and facial expression are affected, and a product framed entirely around walking and stiffness will feel incomplete to anyone in speech therapy. Singing and choir participation sit adjacent to this and are worth offering as activity examples.

Clinical Non-motor
Rock Steady Boxing
帕金森拳击
pàjīnsēn quánjí

A non-contact boxing programme designed specifically for people with PD, run through affiliate gyms internationally. Combines the large, forceful, rhythmic movement that PD training favours with a group setting.

Worth knowing because of what it signals about motivation: participants often describe the community as the reason they keep going, not the exercise. A product that treats a boxing class as one more logged activity misses that it may be the anchor of someone's week. Named programmes like this, tai chi, and dance for PD are good examples to offer in activity prompts — they give permission for the specific and the social rather than the generic "exercise."

Clinical Log design
Tai Chi
太极拳
tàijíquán

A slow, weight-shifting movement practice widely recommended in PD for balance and fall prevention. One of the more studied non-drug interventions, and low-barrier relative to gym-based programmes.

Culturally significant for the project: it is one of the few PD exercise modalities that is already familiar in a Chinese context, which matters if bilingual support returns post-MVP. Also a useful counterexample to the assumption that "activity" means intensity — a form of movement that generates little accelerometer signal but real therapeutic value, which is an argument against relying on passive sensing alone to count sessions.

UX & Product concepts
Chicken Rib Anti-Pattern
鸡肋
jī lèi

From the Three Kingdoms idiom: something that's not worth keeping, but feels wasteful to discard — "tasteless to eat, a pity to throw away." In DoseEgg's design language: any feature that feels like an obligation without delivering clear value. Patients flagged this as a reason to abandon logging.

A core design principle for the product: every feature must earn its place. If a user has to stop and wonder why they're logging something, it's a 鸡肋. The bar for inclusion is whether the insight it generates is worth the friction it creates.

UX principle From research
Schedule vs. Record (plan vs. observation)
计划 vs. 记录
jìhuà vs. jìlù

Two different things the product must never merge. The schedule is the intention: which medications someone takes and roughly when each is meant to happen. The record is what actually happened: the entries, each with a time and a basis. "Plan" is used interchangeably with schedule in the rules — as in "a schedule is a plan, not an observation."

Almost every honesty rule in the product turns on this line. An assumed entry is generated from the schedule and may never render as a taken dose, because nobody observed it. Look Back's dashed rails are the schedule; the dots are the record, and the distance between them is the only thing worth reading. A statement that says "as planned" cannot fire for someone who never entered a schedule, while a statement about spread still can. Practical consequence for setup: the smallest useful schedule is names and times, not doses — times drive the confirmation card, the rails and every Look Back statement, while milligrams drive only the doctor export.

Data model Core distinction
Coverage (the denominator)
覆盖率 · 分母
fùgàilǜ · fēnmǔ

The number of days a statement could actually see, stated alongside the statement itself. "You had a rough afternoon on 5 of the 9 days lunch was close to your dose" — 9 is the coverage. Without it, "5 days" is a rhetorical device rather than a fact: 5 out of 6 and 5 out of 40 are opposite findings that look identical when the denominator is hidden.

A hard rule for the Analyze surface: every claim carries its coverage. Two consequences follow. An entry logged without any depth still counts in the denominator — excluding bare entries would compute every pattern over only the days someone felt well enough to elaborate, biasing the whole product toward good days. And a day with no entry at all is unknown, never zero: it reduces coverage rather than adding a zero to the data. The clinical precedent is the CGM standard in type 1 diabetes, where a report is considered meaningful only at ~14 days and ≥70% data capture — a duration and a coverage floor together.
