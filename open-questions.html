MVP

Analyze / Look Back — the retrospective surface

The third surface alongside Timeline and the doctor export. Opened as an outcome-first workstream in August 2026: the statements a patient would return for were written before any chart, and live in the Analyze statement bank along with the visual language and the rules every statement obeys. Direction: build the retrospective, non-judgmental view first — a rewarding way to see your own record. The pre-appointment / visit-prep framing is the destination, not the first build.

Decided

What is this surface called? — "Look Back" (2026-08-13).

"Analyze" was rejected because it promises inference the first month cannot deliver. "Review" was rejected because it collides with a meaning the product already has — past days are editable, so "review" reads as check and correct your entries. "Look Back" is warmer, carries no second meaning, and survives all three eras of the surface (Recall → Compare → Explain). Nine characters, so it fits a menu row at 200% text.

Open

Should the user be able to include or exclude no-entry days from the denominator?

Tutu's proposal: only the user knows whether a blank day was a hard day or an unbothered one, so let them decide how it counts, with an explanation attached. Three refinements agreed while scoping it. (1) It only affects rate statements ("4 rough afternoons out of 21 days, or out of the 15 you logged?"). It does not affect co-occurrence statements — a day with no entry is neither an X day nor a not-X day, so it is outside that comparison; a global control would imply it changes numbers it cannot. (2) Not a toggle in the chrome — make the coverage line itself the control, so it appears only where it would change the number. (3) The tooltip describes the app's limit, not the user's motive — "some people don't log on hard days" is true of a population and an accusation said to one person; write it as "we can't tell from here." Measuring which setting people choose is behaviour not content, so it is inside the analytics rule — but use it to pick a better global default later, never to silently personalise, or the number changes because the app quietly re-decided how to count.

Future

Chart interactivity and period granularity (D / W / M / 6M, swipe between periods).

Prompted by Apple Health's Sleep screens (Aug 2026). Post-MVP on data volume, but three design positions were settled meanwhile. Do not copy Apple's scrub gesture — dragging a caret is sustained fine motor control, the same interaction v2 already rejected when it replaced the scroll-wheel time picker with ±15m nudges; horizontal swipe between weeks fails for the same reason, and prev/next buttons do the job with a tap. The selectable unit is the day, not the mark — a full-width day row is a comfortable target, whereas seven dots stacked in ~70px cannot carry 48px hit areas; tapping a column and listing its days beats a tooltip and works with a screen reader. Zooming out must change the mark, not shrink it — Apple's own 6M view collapses each week into an earliest-to-latest range bar rather than drawing 180 nights. Also noted: Apple's 6M carries no trendline, arrow, or delta, which is independent support for our annotation rule; and their daily hypnogram is the shape we would want for continuous ON/OFF state if a wearable ever supplies it — reserved, not to be imitated from self-report.

Needs input

"Compare two days side by side" — what did the domain lead mean?

Raised in an earlier sync, never specified; Tutu is following up (tracked in TASKS.md with five questions to probe). Potentially the strongest idea on this surface: it needs exactly two days and the user supplies the hypothesis, so it works in week one — the cold-start problem solved rather than managed — and it is Option A zoomed from seven days to two, so nearly free to build. Position to test: the app renders both days completely and says nothing about the difference, because two days differ in fifty ways and whichever one the app highlights becomes the explanation. Risk to watch: a "good day vs bad day" picker rebuilds the report card [C-08] — pick by date instead.

Open

How much data before the surface says anything — and what does it show below that?

Working answer: the data requirement is a property of each statement, not a global gate on the surface, so the page renders whatever currently qualifies. Rough ladder — rhythm statements from ~6 days, week-over-week comparison from ~3 weeks, co-occurrence from ~6–8 weeks and only with ≥5 days on each side. Below the threshold: the week-in-progress chart filling in, plus a shorter true sentence. Never a locked card, greyed-out preview, countdown, or progress bar toward an insight. Research to do: N-of-1 trial methodology (the domain lead already runs one) and the CGM consensus standard in T1D (~14 days at ≥70% data capture — a duration and a coverage floor; verify the figures before citing).

Open

Does the interval run from the last appointment, or is it a rolling window?

An appointment anchor ("since your last visit") is more meaningful than a rolling 30/90 days and reframes the cold-start problem entirely — but it requires the user to enter a date the app does not currently ask for, and needs a fallback for anyone who never does. It would also be the one legitimate notification trigger, since it fires on a real event rather than on an engagement target.

Open

How many statements at once, in what order, and how does a user dismiss one?

Three at a time feels right; more reads as a dashboard. Ordering by recency and by confidence are both defensible — ordering by past engagement is adaptive and risky, because an adaptive surface can leak a judgment it never states. If a statement can find the user on the Timeline, the user must be able to send it away; what "away" means (this one / this kind / forever) is unresolved.

Open

Should the app ask one targeted question instead of a general plea to log more?

Rather than "add more detail," ask for the single missing fact that would change the most — "was the 14th a walk day?" This is uncertainty sampling from active learning applied to consumer UX: query the highest-information missing label instead of asking for everything. Promising, but it needs a full user story before design — who asks, when, how the user says "I don't remember," what happens to the answer, and what the hard frequency cap is. Uncapped it is 鸡肋 within a fortnight.

Future

Should the app ask permission to read the user's calendar?

Planning is clearly connected to dose and meal timing, and a calendar would supply context that manual logging misses. Parked deliberately (Aug 2026): using it to shape a day edges into medical suggestion, and reading a calendar continuously sits squarely inside the ambient-AI consent bar [C-14]. Revisit only with that framework in place, never as a convenience feature.

Future

Effort-acknowledgment — re-parked August 2026.

Reopened briefly while scoping this surface, and closed again the same day. Two positions were settled in the process and are worth keeping. Praising days logged is rejected outright — it praises app usage rather than health, manufactures a streak, and punishes exactly the weeks someone was too unwell to log. Counting exercise or PT sessions is legitimate, but as clinical evidence rather than praise: count the therapy, never count the app. Also settled: tone must not adapt to outcome — if warmth rises with improvement and flattens with decline, users learn to read tone as diagnosis and silence becomes bad news. Vary content by tenure; hold warmth constant. MVP position unchanged from [C-08]: no encouragement copy at all.

Post-MVP

Smart Symptom Tagging — Learning from Free Logs

If a patient repeatedly describes the same symptom in free-form notes, the app could recognize the pattern and offer it as a quick-log option in the future. Example: a user who often writes "upper body ON, lower body OFF" — over time, this becomes a one-tap entry. The feature reduces logging friction for recurring symptoms without requiring the user to set it up manually. Feature status now tracked in the Feature Backlog; the manual forerunner — custom "+ my own" chips (D-13) — is already in prototype testing, and both prototypes ship a starting library of PD state chips.

Open

How do users describe a symptom in a short, loggable format?

Free-form notes are flexible but hard to pattern-match. Structured tags are scannable but rigid. Is there a middle ground — e.g. a short phrase the user writes once and the app learns to recognize? What does the UX of "naming a symptom" actually look like?

Open

If there are many symptom types, how does a user find and track the right one?

A long list of tags becomes a search problem. Short tags may not carry enough meaning. Long tags are awkward to tap. Options to explore: searchable tag library, user-created tags, AI-suggested tags from note text, or symptom templates based on common PD experiences.

Open

Can the app identify recurring patterns without using an LLM?

Simple frequency counting (same phrase 3+ times → suggest as quick-log) requires no AI and no data leaving the device. Fuzzy matching catches minor variations. Lightweight on-device NLP clustering is more powerful but adds complexity. A full cloud LLM is most capable but raises consent and privacy concerns. Recommendation: start with frequency + fuzzy match, design data model to support ML later.

Future

When does a suggested tag become a permanent part of the user's symptom library?

At some threshold of repetition, the app could promote a free-form note into a named symptom the user can track over time. Who decides — the user, the app, or both? And how does a user retire a symptom that's no longer relevant?

Future

Should the app ship with a starting library of known PD symptoms?

Common PD symptoms (tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, dyskinesia, ON/OFF, freezing of gait, fatigue) could be pre-loaded as quick-log options rather than waiting for the user to describe them. This reduces early friction but may feel clinical or overwhelming if the list is too long.

Exploring

AI Use — Consent, Ethics, and Legal Boundaries

DoseEgg uses AI in at least two distinct ways: on-demand (user initiates a task — photo logging, report generation) and potentially ambient (background analysis of logs to find patterns). These two modes carry very different ethical and legal implications. This area tracks the open questions around how to use AI responsibly, what users need to consent to, and what the app needs to have in place before ambient AI is appropriate.

Needs input

Should onboarding ask users to choose an AI comfort level — and does the "confirm as I go" tier deserve to exist?

Proposed model (July 2026): Tier 1 — block all AI (the app works fully without it; this is the baseline the daily-loop flow is designed against). Tier 2 — full AI experience, permission granted everywhere. Tier 3 — wants the AI-powered experience but confirms where AI is used, via just-in-time prompts at first use of each feature (a tour of AI touchpoints was considered and dropped — tours are forgettable and consent given in a tour is weakly informed). Two mechanisms remain on the table: upfront (choose a tier at onboarding) vs. just-in-time (no onboarding question; per-feature prompts with "don't ask again"). Open validation questions: (1) Can users meaningfully choose a tier at onboarding, before seeing the product? (2) Is tier 2 a real tier, or just tier 3 with all prompts pre-accepted? Decided either way: an AI settings page — every AI touchpoint listed with its current on/off state, so "deny all" / "allow all" is one gesture and individual features are reversible any time. It doubles as the honest disclosure surface the user-agreement question below asks for.

Open

What AI uses require explicit user consent at the moment of use vs. one-time agreement?

On-demand AI (photo logging, report generation) is naturally consent-at-the-moment — the user presses a button. Ambient AI (reading logs continuously to find patterns) is harder: the user agreed once, but are they thinking about it every time they log? Working principle: ambient AI should be an opt-in feature, not on by default, with a clear explanation of what it does and where data goes.

Needs input

Is ambient AI analysis of personal health logs ethical and legal in the US?

Key questions for a health lawyer or privacy expert: (1) Does continuous AI reading of health logs trigger HIPAA obligations? (2) If logs are sent to a third-party AI API (OpenAI, Anthropic), is a Business Associate Agreement required? (3) What does the FTC require around disclosing AI use in consumer health apps? This needs external legal input before building.

Open

On-device vs. cloud AI — which model runs where, and what does the user need to know?

Pattern detection from log frequency can run entirely on-device — no data leaves the phone, no third-party AI. This sidesteps most privacy and HIPAA concerns. Cloud AI (for report generation, semantic understanding) sends data to an external server. Users should know which is which. Design principle: on-device first for passive features; cloud AI only on explicit user action.

Open

What does the user agreement need to say about AI, and how prominent should it be?

Health app users — especially those managing a neurological condition — are often more privacy-aware than typical consumers. Burying AI disclosure in terms of service risks a trust breach when users realize it later. Working principle: be calm but clear. "DoseEgg can optionally learn from your notes to make logging easier over time" is honest without being alarming. The user agreement should state: what AI accesses, when, where data goes, and how to turn it off.

Open

How do we handle AI "noticing" something that feels clinical — without crossing into medical advice?

Pattern detection can surface observations ("you've described upper body stiffness 8 times this month") without interpretation ("your medication may not be working in the afternoon"). The line between observation and interpretation is where liability begins. Design rule: show the user what was noticed, let them decide what it means. Never frame a pattern as a diagnosis or recommendation.

Future

What foundation needs to be in place before ambient AI is appropriate?

At minimum: (1) explicit opt-in with plain-language explanation, (2) on-device vs. cloud distinction made clear, (3) transparency layer — show the user what AI has noticed before acting on it, (4) user agreement with clear AI disclosure, (5) legal review of data flow if using cloud AI APIs. This is a Phase 3 or later question — not relevant until the core logging is built and validated.

MVP

Free-Form Logging

Users can add any freeform note alongside structured logs. This is the lowest-friction way to capture symptoms or context that doesn't fit a predefined field. The data model for free-form notes should be designed with future pattern detection in mind, even if that feature isn't built yet.

Decided

How should free-form notes be stored to support future pattern detection?

Resolved as constraint C-12 (July 2026): discrete timestamped entries, schema reserves fields for token/frequency indexing — see Design Constraints. Original reasoning: flat text blobs are easy to write but hard to analyze later; precise timestamps and discrete entries don't require building pattern detection, they just avoid making it impossible later.

Decided

Is free-form logging in MVP scope?

Yes — and now prototyped (July 2026): the How I feel panel's note field, with the never-discard rule (send / Return / dismissal all save) and mic↔send input. See the Daily Loop Flow. Free-form notes are the escape hatch for anything not covered by structured fields.

Exploring

Data Purpose — Working Data vs. Reporting Data

Research on chronic-illness tracking (Ancker et al. 2015) shows the same log serves different purposes: working data a patient acts on immediately (e.g. a diabetic checks blood glucose and adjusts food or insulin now), data for sense-making (understanding the condition over time), and data for reporting to the doctor. In T1D, glucose is genuinely working data. The open question is what role DoseEgg's logs can play for PD — where ON/OFF is plannable but not reliably predictable.

Open

Can any DoseEgg data become "working data" the user acts on in the moment?

T1D works because glucose is read and dosed against in real time. PD is different — ON/OFF is plannable but unpredictable, so a DoseEgg log is mostly sense-making and doctor-reporting. Is there a narrow exception, e.g. "you tend to go OFF ~90 min after this dose, so plan around it"? That would be working data of a planning kind, not a dosing kind. Worth defining what, if anything, qualifies.

Open

Does chasing prediction / working data push the app toward the optimization framing we're avoiding?

Predictive "do X now" guidance edges toward the dashboard/performance tone DoseEgg deliberately rejects (journal, not report card). Tension to resolve: deliver genuinely useful timing insight without making the user feel they're being scored or must act. Connects to the moral-framing principle — observation over instruction.

Needs input

What does the T1D / CGM ecosystem teach about these three data purposes?

Tied to the queued T1D research task. CGM "time in range" is both working data (act now) and reporting data (clinic review). Studying how those tools separate or blend the two — and how they avoid feeling punitive — should inform DoseEgg's equivalent (e.g. a "time in ON" concept).

Exploring

Input Model — Structured Timeline vs. Conversational Journal

Sparked by Ancker et al.'s "tracking is work" theme. Two possible interaction models: (1) a structured timeline, activity-focused, Oura-like — the user logs events into fields; (2) a conversational journal where the user writes or speaks freely and an AI model distills it into a dry timeline / day summary. The deeper question underneath: a structured UI is biased toward presence ("what did you do?") and may miss the negative space — the dose forgotten, the activity skipped because OFF, the rough stretch never recorded — which is often the richest PD signal. And people log least when sickest, exactly when it matters most.

Open

Will users log negative space — what they did NOT do, or feeling off — not just what they did?

Structured fields capture actions (dose taken, workout done). They don't naturally hold absences: forgot meds, skipped activity, a vague OFF period. A freeform sentence ("couldn't get off the couch till noon") encodes OFF + inactivity + mood at once, at lower effort. Core hypothesis to test: a journal mode captures negative space that a timeline grid misses.

Decided

One app with multiple input depths, rather than two separate models?

Yes — this became the daily-loop design (July 2026): every category has a one-or-two-tap fast path with optional depth, all feeding one timeline — see the Daily Loop Flow. Even a sentence is hard to type mid-OFF, so the lightest tiers matter most. Passive sensing as the lightest tier of all remains open (see Research page, Section 1).

Needs input

AI distilling a journal into a timeline — does it clear the consent/privacy bar?

Partially moved (July 2026): the feature is now spec'd as smart split — user-initiated on a note the user just wrote, behind the C-15 confirm pattern (AI proposes, user confirms, nothing lands unseen) — which is a much easier consent case than ambient journal-reading. See the flow doc's Note section and the Feature Backlog. What remains for legal/expert input: cloud vs. on-device data flow for the parsing itself.

Open

How do we capture "what you didn't do" without it reading as confession?

"You missed your dose" is exactly the "bad patient" moral verdict Ancker warns about. Negative-space capture must stay neutral and observational — the journal framing is gentler than a checklist of misses, but this has to be designed deliberately, not assumed. Ties to the moral-framing principle (observation over judgment).

Exploring

Emotional Design — Coping, Framing & Positive Engagement

A direct recommendation from Ancker et al.: engage with the emotional impact of medical data, not just display it — don't only motivate progress, but help people cope with negative feelings; avoid exacerbating negative judgments; find creative ways to support positive feelings; and facilitate personal goal-setting rather than imposing external goals. Two named technique families: motivational interviewing (help patients set personally relevant goals and action plans, rather than adopting their providers' priorities) and behavioral economics (framing, defaults, and nudges to support engagement and better decisions). This area is the operational extension of the moral-framing principle.

Open

How does the app engage with the emotional weight of data, not just show it?

Data provokes real emotion (the quote bank is full of it). The product should help users cope with a bad stretch, not just register it — and notice and support good moments without it feeling like a reward mechanic. What does "emotional support" look like in a logging app without tipping into therapy-speak or false cheer?

Open

Personal goals vs. external/clinical goals — how does a user set goals that are genuinely theirs?

Motivational-interviewing principle: personally relevant goals beat imposed ones ("for me," not the provider's target — see the A1c "poster child" quote). How does DoseEgg let someone define what "a good day" means to them, rather than shipping a default target? Connects directly to the "good for you, not good" framing.

Needs input

What can motivational interviewing and behavioral economics contribute — applied ethically?

Worth a focused review: MI techniques for goal-setting and action plans; behavioral-economics framing/defaults/nudges for engagement. Tension to hold: nudges and defaults shade into manipulation and optimization pressure fast — the very tone DoseEgg avoids. The question isn't whether these tools work, but which uses respect autonomy vs. quietly steer.

Open

Where's the line between a supportive nudge and the report-card / optimization tone we reject?

A gentle "want to note how the afternoon went?" supports the user; "you're below your streak" judges them. Both are nudges. This block needs a working rule for which side of the line a given pattern sits on. Ties to the no-performance-framing and moral-framing principles.
